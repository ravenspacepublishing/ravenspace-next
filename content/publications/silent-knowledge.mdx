Silent Knowledge explores a contemporary example of Indigenous cultural revival in Sápmi, the Sámi homeland, to investigate issues of embodied cultural memory.

Natalia Magnani draws on ethnographic fieldwork among Sääʹm (Skolt Sámi), a Sámi minority who lived in Arctic territories ceded to the Soviet Union following the Second World War and were subsequently integrated into the Finnish welfare state. She follows a collaboration between Finnish builders and Sääʹm reindeer herders and fishers brought together by a museum and educational project to build a traditional Petsamo root-sewn boat. As these makers adjust their environmental perceptions and relearn barely remembered root-sewing and woodworking techniques, their restoring of material production suggests a “loud” silent, or tacit, knowledge: uncodified skills and wisdom gained through experience that are socially meaningful and politically empowering.

Silent Knowledge unpacks the social and political dynamics of crafting cultural memory for future generations. Magnani demonstrates embodiment as transformation, showing that embodied practice can mediate tensions over who has the right to shape culture by positioning non-Indigenous participants in supporting roles, while keeping Indigenous people firmly at the centre of their own distinct history. The book thus reveals tacit knowledge as an active site of cultural change, political negotiation, and reclamation.

The timely questions raised by this carefully drawn exploration of cultural revival will resonate not only among anthropologists but also among scholars and students in Indigenous studies, Northern studies, material culture studies, and museum studies.
